# Unpacking a claim in conversation — protocol v0.1

**Status:** v0.1, 2026-10-05, drafted from the first three paired analyses in `case-studies/01-ls11-fr11/`. To be revised as cases accumulate.

## The use case

Steve is talking with a climate-sceptical friend. The friend says something; Steve says, roughly, "Interesting — let me write down what I think I heard you say, and let's ask Claude to unpack it a bit." The input is Steve's paraphrase; the output is a short second look that both of them can read at the table.

Three facts about that setting shape everything below.

1. **The elephant is in the room** (Haidt's rider and elephant; Kahneman's System 1 and 2). The friend's first reaction, and Steve's, came from the frame, not from the evidence. A second look works only if it is short, if it does not tell the friend what they *really* think, and if it visibly applies to Steve's side too. Anything that reads as a rebuttal will be heard by the elephant and answered by the rider in the elephant's service.
2. **The input is a paraphrase.** Writing down "what I heard you say" is itself a reframing. The protocol therefore starts by handing the paraphrase back to the friend.
3. **Symmetry is the point, not a courtesy.** The case study found the same frames — deception, authenticity, the alarm bell, signal-and-noise, journey-and-pause — on both sides with the roles swapped. Showing the friend that structure is the single most useful thing the analysis can do; it is also the one thing neither side's own materials will ever show them.

## The procedure

### Step 0 — Fix the paraphrase (at the table, before asking)

Write the claim down in one or two sentences, in the friend's words where possible, and ask: *"Is that fair? Would you change anything?"* Record the friend's edits. This step is both method (the analysis is of a claim the friend owns) and conversation (it signals that being understood comes before being answered).

### Step 1 — Lexical scan (Mode A, quick)

Scan the paraphrase for the words FrameNet covers well — the social and epistemic vocabulary — and name the frame each evokes. From the case study, the ones that recur:

| Word family | FrameNet frame | What the frame carries |
|---|---|---|
| fake, real, genuine, true, false, so-called, "scare quotes" | `Artificiality`, `Being_named` | a contest over who belongs to a category |
| skeptic, skeptical, doubt | `Certainty` | a cognizer's doubt — the honorific both sides claim |
| trick, fool, deceive, mislead, hide, manipulate | `Intentional_deception`, `Prevarication`, `Manipulate_into_doing`, `Hiding_objects` | a Deceiver, a Victim, a Topic — and **intent** |
| clear, obvious, undeniable, evident, show | `Obviousness`, `Evidence` | certainty asserted about the listener as much as the data |
| prove, evidence, suggest, claim | `Evidence`, `Statement` | strength of support — "prove" is the strongest |
| alarming, alarmist, alarm | `Stimulus_focus` (base: `Noise_makers`) | a warning bell, rung truly or falsely |
| pause, stop, slow down, resume | `Activity_pause`, `Process_stop`, `Change_event_duration` | a traveler on a path; a pause presupposes resumption and even an agent |
| confront, attack, arsenal, beat, compete | `Confronting_problem` / `Hostile_encounter`, `Attack`, `Weapon`, `Beat_opponent`, `Competition` | sides, weapons, a scoreboard |
| influence, affect, cause, drive | `Objective_influence`, `Causation` | who or what is acting on what |
| calibrate, adjust, correct | `Adjusting`, `Correctness` | bringing a part into accord with a standard — which presupposes a standard |

The scan takes a minute and its value is that it is blind to allegiance: it catches the loaded word whichever side it comes from. Record gaps too (fit, filter, signal, noise, trend, cycle, model, urgency): those words are carrying a domain script that has to be stated by hand in Step 2.

### Step 2 — The frame that is carrying the argument (Mode B)

Pick the one or two frames or metaphors doing the inferential work, and for each write down:

- **Source → target.** What is being mapped onto what ("circular" → reasoning as a path; "noise" → fluctuations as interference with a message).
- **Entailment pressed.** The inference the speaker wants the listener to draw.
- **Entailments hidden.** What the source domain brings along that does not hold in the target, or what the frame leaves no role for (honest disagreement about a convention; a dependence that is partial rather than total; a number that is a different kind of quantity from the one it is compared with).
- **Construal.** What is profiled and what is backgrounded; where agency is placed ("are fitted to" vs. "we factor out"); what the definite article presupposes ("*the* pause", "*the* real signal").
- **The symmetric instance.** The same frame in use on the other side, quoted. If none can be found, say so — that is itself a finding.

Where the claim rests on a technical script (a decomposition, a comparison of two numbers, a test of a forecast), state the script's roles and the condition under which its inference is licensed (e.g., for subtraction: the "known" components must be known independently of the record).

### Step 3 — Substance, kept separate

Note, in a separate paragraph, what is actually known about the substance — which of the disputed choices are conventions with arguments on both sides, which are decidable and how, and where the evidence is asymmetric. This paragraph is for Steve, not necessarily for the table. Its job is to keep the frame analysis from being read as a verdict, and to keep a verdict from hiding inside a frame analysis.

### Step 4 — The second look (the output)

Three to five sentences, for both people to read. In order:

1. **Name the frame plainly**, as a picture, without the word "frame" ("'Circular' is a path picture: it says the argument comes back where it started").
2. **Grant the real worry underneath it**, in one clause.
3. **Say what the picture adds that the facts don't settle**, and turn it into one or two questions the friend could answer or investigate.
4. **Show the symmetric instance**, in the other side's words, when there is one — framed as information about the word, not about the friend.
5. **Stop.** No verdict, no "but actually," no reading list unless asked.

Write a matching second look for the mainstream version of the claim whenever the analysis is being used in a mixed conversation, and be prepared to read Steve's own side's second look aloud first.

## What the second look is not

- Not a rebuttal. If it answers the claim, the elephant has already left.
- Not a diagnosis of the friend. It describes words, not motives; the deception frames are precisely the ones to avoid *using* while describing them.
- Not neutral about substance. Step 3 exists so that honesty about the evidence is kept, not abandoned — it just is not the job of the three sentences at the table.

## Calibration

The experimental evidence that metaphoric framing shifts reasoning is real but modest and contested (Thibodeau & Boroditsky 2011; the follow-up by Steen, Reijnierse & Burgers 2014; Flusberg, Matlock & Thibodeau 2017 for climate). A second look will not change a mind; its realistic aim is a friend who later notices the word doing the work. That is enough.
