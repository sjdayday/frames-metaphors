# Literature pass — metaphor and framing research on climate discourse

**Status:** v0.1, 2026-10-08. An annotated, verification-tagged reading list for the project, organized by what each item can do for the method, with two framing documents — Luntz 2002 and Lakoff 2009/2010 — and one naming policy — the Guardian's of 2019 — read as primary statements in both modes. Written by Claude at Steve's request from the cloud environment, where most journal pages are closed: the bibliographic records were checked against CrossRef where the API answered (`[V]`), and the summaries of what each paper found are from memory unless a fetched text is cited; each is tagged. A draft to be checked, not a source.

**Verification tags.** `[V]` — the CrossRef record was fetched 2026-10-08 and the title, authors, venue, volume, issue and pages checked against it. `[V-s]` — confirmed only through a secondary or a DOI seen on a citing page. `[K]` — a standard reference from memory, not re-verified; the venue and year are given as remembered and the content summary is marked *(summary from memory)*. `[W]`, `[S]` — fetched text, or a named secondary that transcribes it, as in the case files.

**What the project needs from the literature.** Four things: instruments (frame inventories, metaphor typologies, a vocabulary for naming what the cases find by hand); findings about particular metaphors the cases use (greenhouse, carbon, tipping point, war, religion, pollution, denial); experimental evidence about what wording and metaphor do to readers (which sets the second look's expectations); and studies that treat both sides' language as discourse, which is the symmetry rule's literature. A fifth, which the literature mostly lacks, is the use case: a method for one reader at a table rather than for a public campaign.

## A. Surveys and theory of climate framing

**Nerlich, B., Koteyko, N., & Brown, B. (2010).** Theory and language of climate change communication. *WIREs Climate Change*, 1(1), 97–110. doi:10.1002/wcc.2 `[V]`
A review of the field's three strands — the psychology of framing, the linguistics of metaphor and discourse, and science communication — arguing that climate communication is a cultural politics carried in language, and that metaphors and "carbon compounds" are framing devices with ideological load *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the map this project's two levels sit on; the warrant for treating a lexical choice as a political act on both sides.

**Moser, S. C. (2010).** Communicating climate change: History, challenges, process and future directions. *WIREs Climate Change*, 1(1), 31–53. `[K]`
The history of climate communication and the case against the information-deficit model *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** calibration — the deficit model is what the second look must not be; the elephant does not read the brief.

**Nisbet, M. C. (2009).** Communicating climate change: Why frames matter for public engagement. *Environment: Science and Policy for Sustainable Development*, 51(2), 12–23. `[K]`
An inventory of generalizable frames for science-policy debates applied to climate: social progress, economic development and competitiveness, morality and ethics, scientific and technical uncertainty, Pandora's box / Frankenstein's monster / runaway science, public accountability and governance, middle way / alternative path, conflict and strategy *(list from memory; check the paper's wording)*. **Adds:** a frame inventory that the cases' master frames can be sorted into — Luntz's "scientific uncertainty" frame (E below), Steffen's "Hothouse" as Pandora's box (case 04, G4), the "manufactured" frames as public accountability (case 03, R2), the "contest" and "scoreboard" as conflict and strategy (cases 01–02).

**Hulme, M. (2009).** *Why We Disagree About Climate Change: Understanding Controversy, Inaction and Opportunity.* Cambridge University Press. `[K]`
Climate change as an idea that carries four myths — Lamenting Eden, Presaging Apocalypse, Constructing Babel, Celebrating Jubilee *(from memory)* — and the argument that disagreement is about values, not evidence. **Adds:** master narratives for the narrative level (glossary E); the Apocalypse and Eden myths are the two ends of case 04's benefit/catastrophe axis.

**Lakoff, G. (2010).** Why it matters how we frame the environment. *Environmental Communication*, 4(1), 70–81. doi:10.1080/17524030903529749 `[K]`; keywords fetched from a preview: "Framing; the Real; Messaging; Enlightenment Reason; Hypocognition; Global Warming." `[W-x]`
The academic version of the 2009 essay read in E below: frames as neural circuits; "hypocognition" (the lack of frames for what the environment is); the "environment" frame that separates people from nature; systemic against direct causation; the claim that facts without frames do not move the public *(summary from memory and from the 2009 version)*. **Adds:** the theory this project uses, stated by its author as advice to one side — which is why it is also a primary statement.

## B. Metaphor studies of climate discourse

**Romaine, S. (1996).** War and peace in the global greenhouse: Metaphors we die by. *Metaphor and Symbolic Activity*, 11(3), 175–194. doi:10.1207/s15327868ms1103_1 `[V]`
An early analysis of the metaphors of the greenhouse debate — the greenhouse itself, war, and the planet as a body or patient — with the Lakoff–Johnson title turned to the point that some metaphors have consequences for survival *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the first treatment of the metaphor case 04 traces; a prior for the finding that both sides reason inside the greenhouse picture.

**Nerlich, B., & Koteyko, N. (2009).** Compounds, creativity and complexity in climate change communication: The case of "carbon indulgences". *Global Environmental Change*, 19(3), 345–353. doi:10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2009.03.001 `[V]`
The "carbon compounds" (carbon footprint, carbon diet, carbon sinner, carbon indulgence) as lexical creativity that carries frames; the religious frame (indulgences) used to criticize offsetting *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the family behind case 04's "carbon pollution" and the sceptical "carbon is the stuff of life" — the metonym's compounds, each a frame; and a mainstream-side religious frame to set beside the sceptical side's (case 03, R3).

**Koteyko, N., Thelwall, M., & Nerlich, B. (2010).** From carbon markets to carbon morality: Creative compounds as framing devices in online discourses on climate change mitigation. *Science Communication*, 32(1), 25–54. doi:10.1177/1075547009340421 `[V]`
A large-scale study of carbon compounds online, sorted into finance (carbon market, carbon credit), lifestyle (carbon footprint, carbon diet) and attitude/morality (carbon guilt, carbon sinner) frames, with the drift from the first to the third over time *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** a worked typology of how one metonym ("carbon") breeds frames — the model for a possible "carbon" case; and the finding that the moral register grew, which is the mainstream mirror of the sceptical side's "religion" charge.

**Nerlich, B. (2010).** "Climategate": Paradoxical metaphors and political paralysis. *Environmental Values*, 19(4), 419–442. `[K]`
The metaphors of the 2009 e-mail affair in the blogosphere — the e-mails as a smoking gun, a nail in the coffin, the "greatest scandal", and the paradox that the sceptical side's metaphors of exposure borrowed the science's own metaphors of evidence *(summary from memory; check)*. **Adds:** a study of the sceptical blogosphere's metaphors by a mainstream-aligned linguist — the asymmetry of attention the symmetry rule corrects — and material for case 03's "manufactured" cluster.

**Nerlich, B., & Jaspal, R. (2012).** Metaphors we die by? Geoengineering, metaphors, and the argument from catastrophe. *Metaphor and Symbol*, 27(2), 131–147. doi:10.1080/10926488.2012.665795 `[V]`
The metaphors of geoengineering — the planet as a patient, the planet as a machine, "Plan B", the thermostat — and the "argument from catastrophe" that licenses them *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the machine and thermostat frames case 04 finds in Lacis 2010 and Steffen 2018, and the structure of the catastrophe argument (G4).

**Shaw, C., & Nerlich, B. (2015).** Metaphor as a mechanism of global climate change governance: A study of international policies, 1992–2012. *Ecological Economics*, 109, 34–40. doi:10.1016/j.ecolecon.2014.11.001 `[V]`
The metaphors in two decades of international climate-policy texts, and the argument that a small set of them structured which governance options could be thought — the problem as a quantity to be managed, a pathway, a target *(summary from memory; check the paper's own list)*. **Adds:** the policy register's metaphors, which this project has not yet analysed; the UNFCCC's "degree zero" definition (case 04, T-17) sits inside a document whose other sentences are not metaphor-free.

**Russill, C. (2008).** Tipping point forewarnings in climate change communication: Some implications of an emerging trend. *Environmental Communication*, 2(2), 133–153. doi:10.1080/17524030802141711 `[V]`
**Russill, C., & Nyssa, Z. (2009).** The tipping point trend in climate change communication. *Global Environmental Change*, 19(3), 336–344. doi:10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2009.04.001 `[V]`
The rise of "tipping point" in climate communication from about 2005, its sources (Gladwell's popularization; epidemiology; Lenton's formalization), its entailments (suddenness, irreversibility, a single point) and the contest over it *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the trace of a second foundational metaphor, done in the literature; the entailments list for case 04's "dominoes" and "cascade" (G4); with van der Hel et al. 2018 (in `references.md`), the model for a "tipping point" case.

**Atanasova, D., & Koteyko, N. (2017).** Metaphors in Guardian Online and Mail Online opinion-page content on climate change: War, religion, and politics. *Environmental Communication*, 11(4), 452–469. doi:10.1080/17524032.2015.1024705 `[V]`
Metaphors in the opinion pages of a left and a right British paper: war, religion and politics on both, with religion used by the Mail to frame climate science as a faith and by the Guardian to frame denial as a creed, and war used by both *(summary from memory; check the direction of each finding)*. **Adds:** the symmetry rule's closest published relative — the same source domains on both sides, in the same genre — and a check on cases 02–03's religion and war findings.

**Deignan, A., Semino, E., & Paul, S.-A. (2019).** Metaphors of climate science in three genres: Research articles, educational texts, and secondary school student talk. *Applied Linguistics*, 40(2), 379–403. doi:10.1093/applin/amx035 `[V]`
How the metaphors of climate science change by genre: in research articles "greenhouse", "sink" and "forcing" are technical terms used without elaboration; educational texts elaborate them into pictures (the blanket, the trap, the greenhouse with its glass); students then reason from the pictures, sometimes literally, producing misconceptions *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the published version of case 04's trace — from the technical literature into public use, by genre — and evidence for its main finding: the elaborated picture is where the wrong inferences come from.

**Larson, B. (2011).** *Metaphors for Environmental Sustainability: Redefining Our Relationship with Nature.* Yale University Press. `[K]`
A biologist's survey of the metaphors science lends to environmental discourse (feedback, invasive species, the health of the planet, the greenhouse) and their social consequences *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the scientist's-eye view of metaphors as instruments with side effects.

**Semino, E. (2008).** *Metaphor in Discourse.* Cambridge University Press. `[K]`
The general method behind B — identification, patterns, functions — of which the climate studies are applications.

## C. Wording and metaphor experiments

**Schuldt, J. P., Konrath, S. H., & Schwarz, N. (2011).** "Global warming" or "climate change"? Whether the planet is warming depends on question wording. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 75(1), 115–124. `[K]`
In a U.S. survey, Republicans were less likely to say the phenomenon is real when the question said "global warming" than "climate change"; Democrats were unaffected *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the experimental mirror of the Luntz memo (E): the term the memo chose as "less frightening" is the one the memo's own audience accepts more — a wording effect with a partisan sign.

**Villar, A., & Krosnick, J. A. (2011).** Global warming vs. climate change, taxes vs. prices: Does word choice matter? *Climatic Change*, 105(1–2), 1–12. `[K]`
Found small or no effects of "global warming" vs "climate change" on seriousness ratings across U.S. and European samples, and some effect of "tax" vs "price" *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the other result — wording effects are real but inconsistent — which is the calibration the protocol already states.

**Whitmarsh, L. (2009).** What's in a name? Commonalities and differences in public understanding of "climate change" and "global warming". *Public Understanding of Science*, 18(4), 401–420. `[K]`
U.K. respondents associated "global warming" with heat and human causation and "climate change" with natural variation and a range of impacts *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the two names evoke different frames, which is what the naming scale predicts; and the direction-of-cause difference is the one the Luntz memo was after.

**Hardisty, D. J., Johnson, E. J., & Weber, E. U. (2010).** A dirty word or a dirty world? Attribute framing, political affiliation, and query theory. *Psychological Science*, 21(1), 86–92. `[K]`
The same surcharge labelled "carbon tax" or "carbon offset": Republicans and independents preferred the offset-labelled option, Democrats were indifferent *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** a label as a frame with a party-conditional effect; the "carbon" compound (B) in an experiment.

**Spence, A., & Pidgeon, N. (2010).** Framing and communicating climate change: The effects of distance and outcome frame manipulations. *Global Environmental Change*, 20(4), 656–667. `[K]`
Gain frames produced more positive attitudes to mitigation than loss frames; distance (local vs. distant impacts) had modest effects *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** calibration; the "benefit" frame of case 04 (G3) has an experimental side.

**Thibodeau, P. H., & Boroditsky, L. (2011)** and **Flusberg, S. J., Matlock, T., & Thibodeau, P. H. (2017)** — in `references.md` (metaphor-framing experiments). Flusberg et al.: a "war" metaphor for climate change raised urgency and willingness to act relative to a "race" metaphor *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the one experiment on a climate metaphor's effect; it is the mainstream's "war" frame (case 03, R2's "ammunition") that was tested, not the sceptical side's.

**Thibodeau, P. H., Frantz, C. M., & Berretta, M. (2017).** The earth is our home: Systemic metaphors to redefine our relationship with nature. *Environmental Communication*, 11(4), 429–443. `[K]` (volume and pages from memory)
Tested "home"/"body" style systemic metaphors for nature against mechanistic ones *(summary from memory; check)*. **Adds:** Lakoff's "we are part of nature" (E) as a tested frame.

**Feinberg, M., & Willer, R. (2011).** Apocalypse soon? Dire messages reduce belief in global warming by contradicting just-world beliefs. *Psychological Science*, 22(1), 34–38. `[K]`
Dire messages reduced belief in warming among those high in just-world beliefs *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** evidence behind the "alarmist" second look (cases 01–04): the catastrophe register has a measured backfire.

**O'Neill, S., & Nicholson-Cole, S. (2009).** "Fear won't do it": Promoting positive engagement with climate change through visual and iconic representations. *Science Communication*, 30(3), 355–379. `[K]`
Fear-based imagery attracts attention and reduces engagement *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the same point for images; the "alarmist" label (case 03, R4) names a real effect, which does not make the labelled statement false.

**Kahan, D. M., Peters, E., Wittlin, M., et al. (2012).** The polarizing impact of science literacy and numeracy on perceived climate change risks. *Nature Climate Change*, 2, 732–735. `[K]`
Polarization on climate risk increases with science literacy and numeracy *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the limit the README names: a second look is a cue to a rider whose elephant has already chosen; the more skilled the rider, the better the rationalization. The use case — one friend, one table — is the setting in which this finding is least discouraging, because identity is not on display.

## D. Both sides as discourse

**Hulme, M. (2008).** The conquering of climate: Discourses of fear and their dissolution. *Geographical Journal*, 174(1), 5–16. `[K]`
A climate scientist's critique of the catastrophe discourse from inside the mainstream *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the mainstream's own "alarmist" second look; with Risbey, the pair that shows "alarmist" is contested within the field, not only across it.

**Risbey, J. S. (2008).** The new climate discourse: Alarmist or alarming? *Global Environmental Change*, 18(1), 26–37. `[K]`
Distinguishes "alarmist" (unwarranted by the evidence) from "alarming" (warranted), and argues that much of the strong language of the mid-2000s was the second *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the naming scale for the "alarm" category (case 03, R4): ALARMING (proportioned) → ALARMIST (not) — the distinction the label erases.

**Oreskes, N., & Conway, E. M. (2010).** *Merchants of Doubt.* Bloomsbury; **Dunlap, R. E., & McCright, A. M. (2011).** Organized climate change denial. In *The Oxford Handbook of Climate Change and Society*; **Brulle, R. J. (2014).** Institutionalizing delay: Foundation funding and the creation of U.S. climate change counter-movement organizations. *Climatic Change*, 122, 681–694. `[K]` (all three)
The documentary and sociological literature on the organized sceptical movement *(summaries from memory)*. **Adds:** the substance behind case 03's "manufactured doubt" (R2) — documented for organizations and funders, which is not the same population as the scientists quoted in cases 01–04; the symmetry rule's reminder that a discourse study of one side is a study of one side.

**Carvalho, A. (2007).** Ideological cultures and media discourses on scientific knowledge: Re-reading news on climate change. *Public Understanding of Science*, 16(2), 223–243. `[K]`
British broadsheets' coverage read through their ideological cultures *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** the finding that the same science is framed by outlet, which the Guardian/Mail metaphor study (B) later quantified.

**Boykoff, M. T. (2011).** *Who Speaks for the Climate? Making Sense of Media Reporting on Climate Change.* Cambridge University Press; **Boykoff, M. T., & Boykoff, J. M. (2004).** Balance as bias: Global warming and the US prestige press. *Global Environmental Change*, 14(2), 125–136. `[K]`
The "balance" norm as a frame that overstates dissent *(summary from memory)*. **Adds:** a meta-frame on the mainstream's side (the press as a distorting relay — Koonin's "telephone", case 03) with the opposite sign.

**Lewandowsky, S., et al. (2015)** on "seepage" — in case 02; the mainstream's meta-frame for the other direction of influence.

## E. Framing documents read as primary statements

The theory in A is, in two of its sources, also advice to one side. That makes the advice a statement to analyse. Three documents are paired here by job.

### E.1 The texts

**Luntz 2002** — Luntz Research Companies, "The Environment: A Cleaner, Safer, Healthier America," a memo to Republican candidates, written in 2002 and reported in March 2003. `[S]` — quoted through Burkeman, *The Guardian*, 4 March 2003 (as reposted by CorpWatch) and through Skeptical Science's "Climate Change: the Terminological Timeline", both fetched 2026-10-08; the memo PDF (once at ewg.org) was not reachable.

> The terminology in the upcoming environmental debate needs refinement, starting with "global warming" and ending with "environmentalism." […] It's time for us to start talking about "climate change" instead of global warming and "conservation" instead of preservation. […] "Climate change" is less frightening than "global warming." […] (a focus-group participant:) sounds like you're going from Pittsburgh to Fort Lauderdale. […] While global warming has catastrophic connotations attached to it, climate change suggests a more controllable and less emotional challenge. (via Skeptical Science)

> The scientific debate is closing [against us] but not yet closed. There is still a window of opportunity to challenge the science. […] Voters believe that there is no consensus about global warming within the scientific community. Should the public come to believe that the scientific issues are settled, their views about global warming will change accordingly. Therefore, you need to continue to make the lack of scientific certainty a primary issue in the debate. […] A compelling story, even if factually inaccurate, can be more emotionally compelling than a dry recitation of the truth (via Burkeman 2003)

**Luntz 2006** — interview for *Frontline*, "Hot Politics," 13 November 2006 (posted 24 April 2007). `[W]`

> Global warming is more frightening to the public. […] By 2000 the scientific consensus actually was not uncertain. […] my own beliefs have changed from when I was tasked with that project. […] Today I would not have that paragraph in a document I put out about the environment or about energy

**Lakoff 2009** — George Lakoff, "Why Environmental Understanding, or 'Framing,' Matters," *Huffington Post* / AlterNet, May 2009 (the essay that became the 2010 *Environmental Communication* article). `[W]` — fetched 2026-10-08; the page shows no byline, and the attribution is from Ivakhiv's 2009 discussion of it `[S]`.

[…] All of our language is defined in terms of our frame-circuitry. […] But if our language does not fit our frame circuitry, it will not be understood, or will be misunderstood. (via Ivakhiv)

[…] Second, the economic and ecological meltdowns have the same cause: the unregulated free market […] The result has been deadly, toxic assets and a toxic atmosphere. ### E.2 Pair F1 — How to frame: Lakoff 2009 and Luntz 2002

**The job.** Each document tells its side how to speak so that the public will come to its view. Each is written by a professional of language (a cognitive linguist; a pollster) and each rests on the same theory: that the public is moved by frames and stories before facts — "if our language does not fit our frame circuitry, it will not be understood" (Lakoff); "A compelling story, even if factually inaccurate, can be more emotionally compelling than a dry recitation of the truth" (Luntz).

**Mode A, briefly.** Luntz: *frightening* (`Experiencer_focus` family: the public as experiencer of fear), *catastrophic connotations* (`Catastrophe`), *controllable* (`Control`), *emotional* (gap), *refinement* (of terminology), *window of opportunity* (`Time_period_of_action` — FrameNet's own LU), *challenge the science* (`Hostile_encounter` / `Taking_sides`), *settled* (`Make_agreement_on_action`, case 03), *sound science* (not in the fetched fragments; the memo's phrase as remembered, `[K]`). Lakoff: *frames* (gap in the FrameNet sense, which is the joke), *neural circuit* (`Gizmo`-adjacent; the brain as wiring), *evil* (`Morality_evaluation`), *toxic* (`Toxic_substance` — case 04's poison frame for the atmosphere and for assets), *meltdown* (a reactor), *we are the polar bears* (an identification), *own the air jointly* (`Possession`), *threatened* (`Endangering`), *paradigm* (gap). The lexicon sorts them: Luntz's words are about the audience's state (fear, emotion, control) and about contest (challenge, window); Lakoff's are about moral kinds (evil, toxic) and systems (cause, circuit, meltdown).

**Mode B.** The two documents are one instrument with the sign reversed. Both treat a name as a lever: Luntz would move the public from "global warming" to "climate change" because the second is "less frightening" and "more controllable"; the Guardian (F2) would move it from "climate change" to "crisis" because the first is "passive and gentle". Both treat the public as framed before it is informed, and both say so in a document meant for their own side, not for the public — which is why each reads, when leaked or published, as a confession. Both claim accuracy for the chosen word: Luntz's memo invokes "sound science" (`[K]`) and the Guardian's "scientifically precise"; Lakoff's "the Real" is in his keywords. The asymmetries are in content, not in method: Luntz's advice was to prolong a dispute the memo itself says is "closing [against us]" — an instruction to frame against the author's own assessment of the evidence, which Luntz disowned in 2006 ("Today I would not have that paragraph"); Lakoff's advice is to frame in the direction of what he takes the evidence to show, and its weakest element is the moral absolute ("The destructive exploitation of nature is evil"), which hands the other side the religion frame (cases 02–03) as a gift. A second look at either document says the same thing: *this is a frame chosen for its effect; the author tells you so; ask what the evidence says and then ask why the word was chosen.*

**Scenario / narrative.** Luntz: a campaign (a "window of opportunity", a "battle" the side has "lost", a debate to keep open); the villain is the environmentalist "extremist", the victim the voter who believes "there is no consensus", the remedy the better word. Lakoff: a system in meltdown; the villain is the unregulated market and "the Right's economic arguments", the victim "the polar bears" (us), the remedy a changed understanding of nature. Each narrative is the kind the other side names: Luntz's is the "playbook" (case 03, M-13) and Lakoff's is the "alarmist" (case 01, P2) — and each author would accept the description of his method while rejecting the description of his motive.

### E.3 Pair F2 — What to call it: Luntz's "climate change" and the Guardian's "climate crisis"

**The job.** A naming instruction for the same phenomenon, issued by each side to its own writers, with the reason stated.

**The naming scale** (the protocol's instrument, case 02): GLOBAL WARMING (a direction, a quantity, a cause implied by the word "warming"; "catastrophic connotations" — Luntz) → CLIMATE CHANGE (a direction unspecified; "more controllable and less emotional" — Luntz; "passive and gentle" — Viner) → GLOBAL HEATING (the direction, with an agent implied by "heating" — "more scientifically accurate", the Guardian) → CLIMATE CRISIS / EMERGENCY (a verdict about seriousness and time) → CLIMATE BREAKDOWN (a verdict about the system). Each side chose its point on the scale by the reaction it wanted — less fear, more urgency — and each said so, which is rare and useful: the Luntz memo and the Guardian announcement are the two documents in the whole corpus in which the naming is done out loud, with the emotional target named. The two are symmetric in method and in candour; they differ in what they take the evidence to warrant.

**What the experiments say** (C). Schuldt et al. found the Luntz effect, with a partisan sign — Republicans accepted "climate change" more readily than "global warming"; Villar & Krosnick found little; Whitmarsh found the two names evoke different causes. The wording effect is real, modest, and conditional on who is listening — the protocol's calibration sentence again.

**The label change.** "Climate sceptic" → "climate science denier" is case 03's R4 written into a style guide, with the reason given: "Most 'climate sceptics', in the face of overwhelming scientific evidence, deny climate change is happening." The reason is a claim about the population, which the survey record (case 03, Figure R-1 B) answers: the surveyed minority mostly disputes the human share, not the existence. The style guide's "the original terms are not banned" is the frame concession's mirror: the old word kept, in quotation marks.

**Second look, either direction:** "Both sides have written down what to call it and why: one chose 'climate change' because it is less frightening, the other 'climate crisis' because 'climate change' is too gentle. Each picked a word for its effect on you, and each said so. The scale runs from 'warming' through 'change' to 'crisis' and 'breakdown', and the evidence sits at a point on it that the assessments state in likelihoods, not in names — 'extremely likely', 'unequivocal', with ranges for what is open. Which word would you choose for the thing the ladder describes, and what does your choice say about what you want the listener to feel?"

## F. What the literature adds to the method

1. **A frame inventory.** Nisbet's eight frames and Hulme's four myths give names for the master frames the cases found by hand (contest, uncertainty, Pandora's box, accountability, Eden, Apocalypse). The glossary's narrative level can cite them.
2. **The carbon compounds.** Koteyko, Thelwall & Nerlich's typology is a model for tracing a metonym's family; "carbon pollution" and "carbon is the stuff of life" (case 04, G3) are its two ends.
3. **Genre as the trace's axis.** Deignan, Semino & Paul show that the elaborated picture (blanket, trap, glass) lives in the educational register and the misconceptions come from there — the published form of case 04's main finding, and the reason the second look must offer a picture rather than a correction.
4. **Both sides, same source domains.** Atanasova & Koteyko's war/religion/politics on both sides is the symmetry rule's closest published relative; the cases' mirrored pairs (protocol v0.3) have a literature.
5. **Calibration.** The wording and metaphor experiments find effects that are real, modest, and conditional on the listener's identity (Schuldt; Hardisty; Villar & Krosnick; Flusberg); dire messages can backfire (Feinberg & Willer; O'Neill & Nicholson-Cole); literacy polarizes (Kahan). A second look is a cue, not a lever — the README's sentence, now with sources.
6. **Alarming against alarmist.** Risbey's distinction is the naming scale for the "alarm" label, and Hulme 2008 shows the scale is used inside the mainstream too.
7. **The framing documents are the best primaries.** Luntz 2002 and the Guardian 2019 are the two statements in which a side names its word and its emotional target in the same sentence. The protocol's Step 0 ("fix the paraphrase") can add: *ask whether the friend's word is one a side chose on purpose, and whether the choosing is on the record.*

## G. Gaps, and what this project could contribute

- The studies in B and D analyse one side at a time more often than both, and the mainstream side less often than the sceptical one when the method is critical; the symmetry rule — same instruments, pairs by job, substance kept separate — is a methodological contribution the literature does not clearly have.
- No study found here annotates climate statements with FrameNet. The gaps catalogue (technical and institutional vocabulary absent; social and epistemic vocabulary present) is a finding about the lexicon as much as about the discourse.
- The second look as an intervention is untested. A Thibodeau-style experiment — a paraphrased claim, with and without a three-sentence second look, measured on the reader's later recognition of the word doing the work — would say whether the use case has an effect. Kahan's result predicts it would be small and identity-conditional; the one-table setting is where it should be largest.
- The policy register (Shaw & Nerlich) and the "carbon" family (Koteyko et al.) are the two metaphor fields this project has not yet entered; "fingerprint" (the attribution metaphor; case 05 if wanted) and "tipping point" (Russill; van der Hel) are the two foundational metaphors with a literature to build on.
