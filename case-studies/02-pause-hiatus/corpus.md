# Case study 02 — corpus: the "pause", the "hiatus", the slowdown, 2006–2018

**Status:** v0.1, 2026-10-06. Assembled unattended from sources reachable from the cloud environment plus the verified case-01 captures; items marked `[S]` were quoted through a secondary source because the primary was blocked, and should be checked against the primary before they are relied on for anything beyond the wording of the frame.

## What this corpus is

Statements, from both sides, about the period of slower surface warming after the 1998 El Niño — what to call it, what it shows about the models, how to measure it, and who adopted whose word for it. Unlike case 01 this is not one dispute between two papers but a decade of exchanges, so the corpus is a selection: the statements that named the thing, the statements that drew the inference, and the statements that later argued about the naming itself.

The scientific record is in `climate-science-evolution/02-problems/P08-observational-record.md` (§"The 1998–2012 slowdown (2009–2017)", status `RESOLVED` 2015–17) and is not re-argued here.

## Sides

`S-n`: statements from the sceptical side (and from outlets carrying them). `M-n`: statements from the mainstream side — scientists, assessments, agencies — including the mainstream's adoption of the word and its later self-criticism. The sides are about *who is speaking*, not about who is right; several M statements are mainstream scientists disagreeing with each other.

## M side

**M-1** — Foster ("Tamino"), *Open Mind*, 6 December 2011. `[C]` (case 01, FR-1, FR-4, FR-5, FR-15)

> […] fluctuations which are often exploited by fake skeptics to suggest that global warming has paused, or slowed down, or isn't happening at all.

> This has spurred numerous false claims of silly things like "global warming stopped in 1998" […] leading to meaningless statements about "no statistically significant warming for 15 years" (or 10 years, or 7, or since last Thursday).

> All five data sets show statistically significant warming since 2000.

**M-2** — Rahmstorf, IOP press release, 5 December 2011, and *KlimaLounge*, 8 December 2011. `[C]` (case 01, FR-7, FR-9)

> Our approach shows that the idea that the global warming trend has slowed or even paused over the last decade or so is a groundless misconception.

> Also auch hier keinerlei Hinweise auf eine Verlangsamung oder gar Pause in der globalen Erwärmung. / *So here too, no indication whatsoever of a slowdown, let alone a pause, in global warming.*

**M-3** — UK Met Office, *The recent pause in global warming (2): What are the potential causes?*, July 2013 (one of three reports under the series title "The recent pause in global warming"). `[W]` from the Met Office PDF.

> Global mean surface temperatures rose rapidly from the 1970s, but there has been little further warming over the most recent 10 to 15 years to 2013.

> It is not possible to explain the recent lack of surface warming solely by reductions in the total energy received by the planet, i.e. the balance between the total solar energy entering the system and the thermal energy leaving it.

(Carbon Brief, 25 July 2013, quotes the companion report: "Global mean surface temperatures rose rapidly from the 1970s, but have been relatively flat over the most recent 15 years to 2013" and "[T]he prospects of substantial global warming by the end of the century are not materially altered by the recent pause in global surface warming." `[S]`)

**M-4** — IPCC AR5 WG1 Summary for Policymakers, September 2013, §B.1 and §D.1. `[S]` — the IPCC servers were unreachable from here; the sentences are quoted through RealClimate (Rahmstorf, 17 December 2013), the Grantham Institute's Briefing Note 1 (Buckle & MacTavish, September 2013) and Homewood (S-3), which agree on the wording; the bracketed ranges are from the Grantham note.

> Due to natural variability, trends based on short records are very sensitive to the beginning and end dates and do not in general reflect long-term climate trends.

> As one example, the rate of warming over the past 15 years (1998–2012; 0.05 [–0.05 to 0.15] °C per decade), which begins with a strong El Niño, is smaller than the rate calculated since 1951 (1951–2012; 0.12 [0.08 to 0.14] °C per decade).

> (§D.1, as given by the Grantham note:) The recent observed slowdown in the temperature trend for 1998-2012 relative to 1951-2012 is probably attributable in roughly equal measure to a surface cooling contribution from ocean-atmosphere internal variability and a reduced trend in the external forcing.

**M-5** — IPCC AR5 WG1 Chapter 9, Box 9.2 ("Climate Models and the Hiatus in Global Mean Surface Warming of the Past 15 Years"), 2013. `[S]` via Thorne, Outten, Bethke & Seland (2015), *JGR Atmospheres*, "Investigating the recent apparent hiatus in surface temperature increases: 2," which quotes it.

> In summary, the observed recent warming hiatus, defined as the reduction in GMST trend during 1998–2012 as compared to the trend during 1951–2012, is attributable in roughly equal measure to a cooling contribution from internal variability and a reduced trend in external forcing (expert judgment, medium confidence).

**M-6** — Kosaka & Xie (2013), *Nature*, title, and Karl et al. (2015), *Science*, title, as cited in `climate-science-evolution/02-problems/P08`. `[P-08]`-type citation of titles.

> Recent global-warming hiatus tied to equatorial Pacific surface cooling

> Possible artifacts of data biases in the recent global surface warming hiatus

**M-7** — Richard Muller, "A Pause, Not an End, to Warming," *New York Times* op-ed, 2013, as quoted on Berkeley Earth's "Global Warming Pause?" page. `[S]`

> The global warming crowd has a problem. For all of its warnings, and despite a steady escalation of greenhouse gas emissions into the atmosphere, the planet's average surface temperature has remained pretty much the same for the last 15 years.

**M-8** — Stefan Rahmstorf, "The global temperature jigsaw," *RealClimate*, 17 December 2013. `[W]`

> Since 1998 the global temperature has risen more slowly than before.

> The long-term warming and the short-term "pause" have nothing to do with each other, since they have very different causes.

> I think (just like Trenberth) that natural variability, in particular ENSO and PDO, is the main reason the recent slower warming.

> A central point here: even if the surface temperature stagnates our planet continues to take up heat.

**M-9** — Lewandowsky, Oreskes, Risbey, Newell & Smithson (2015), "Seepage: Climate change denial and its effect on the scientific community," *Global Environmental Change* 33, 1–13, abstract. `[W]` from the UWA repository record.

> Vested interests and political agents have long opposed political or regulatory action in response to climate change by appealing to scientific uncertainty. Here we examine the effect of such contrarian talking points on the scientific community itself. […] We illustrate the consequences of seepage from public debate into the scientific process with a case study involving the interpretation of temperature trends from the last 15 years.

**M-10** — Fyfe, Meehl, England et al. (2016), "Making sense of the early-2000s warming slowdown," *Nature Climate Change* 6, 224–228, opening. `[W]`

> It has been claimed that the early-2000s global warming slowdown or hiatus, characterized by a reduced rate of global surface warming, has been overstated, lacks sound scientific basis, or is unsupported by observations. The evidence presented here contradicts these claims.

**M-11** — Lewandowsky, Risbey & Oreskes (2016), "The 'pause' in global warming: Turning a routine fluctuation into a problem for science," *BAMS* 97(5), 723–733, abstract. `[W]` from the UWA repository record.

> There has been much recent published research about a putative 'pause' or 'hiatus' in global warming. We show that there are frequent fluctuations in the rate of warming around a longer-term warming trend, and that there is no evidence that identifies the recent period as unique or particularly unusual. In confirmation, we show that the notion of a pause in warming is considered to be misleading in a blind expert test. Nonetheless, the most recent fluctuation about the longer-term trend has been regarded by many as an explanatory challenge that climate science must resolve. This departs from long-standing practice, insofar as scientists have long recognized that the climate fluctuates, that linear increases in CO2 do not produce linear trends in global warming, and that 15-yr (or shorter) periods are not diagnostic of long-term trends. We suggest that the repetition of the 'warming has paused' message by contrarians was adopted by the scientific community in its problem-solving and answer-seeking role and has led to undue focus on, and mislabeling of, a recent fluctuation. We present an alternative framing that could have avoided inadvertently reinforcing a misleading claim.

**M-12** — Medhaug, Stolpe, Fischer & Knutti (2017), "Reconciling controversies about the 'global warming hiatus'," *Nature* 545, 41–47, abstract. `[W]`

> Between about 1998 and 2012, a time that coincided with political negotiations for preventing climate change, the surface of Earth seemed hardly to warm. This phenomenon, often termed the 'global warming hiatus', caused doubt in the public mind about how well anthropogenic climate change and natural variability are understood. Here we show that apparently contradictory conclusions stem from different definitions of 'hiatus' and from different datasets. A combination of changes in forcing, uptake of heat by the oceans, natural variability and incomplete observational coverage reconciles models and data. Combined with stronger recent warming trends in newer datasets, we are now more confident than ever that human influence is dominant in long-term warming.

**M-13** — NOAA Climate.gov, "Did global warming stop in 1998?", Rebecca Lindsey, 4 September 2018 (reviewed by Trenberth, Donner, Rood, Medhaug). `[W]`

> No, but thanks to natural variability, volcanic eruptions, and relatively low solar activity, the rate of average global surface warming from 1998-2012 was slower than it had been for two to three decades leading up to it.

> Those who deny the scientific evidence of human-caused global warming turned the slowdown into a slogan: 'Global warming stopped in 1998.' In scientific journals and assessment reports, climate experts described the episode as a 'pause' or 'hiatus' in the previous decades' rapid warming: they knew it wouldn't last.
