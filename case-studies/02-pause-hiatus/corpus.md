# Case study 02 — corpus: the "pause", the "hiatus", the slowdown, 2006–2018

**Status:** v0.2, 2026-10-07. v0.1 was assembled unattended from sources reachable from the cloud environment plus the verified case-01 captures. v0.2 replaces four secondary quotations with the primaries Steve supplied as printed PDFs (Koonin 2014; Carter 2006; Monckton 2017; Monbiot 2010 quoting Rose 2010) and adds the material they contain. Items still marked `[S]` (the AR5 SPM sentences, Rose's 2012 headline, Muller's op-ed) should be checked against their primaries before they are relied on for anything beyond the wording of the frame.

## What this corpus is

Statements, from both sides, about the period of slower surface warming after the 1998 El Niño — what to call it, what it shows about the models, how to measure it, and who adopted whose word for it. Unlike case 01 this is not one dispute between two papers but a decade of exchanges, so the corpus is a selection: the statements that named the thing, the statements that drew the inference, and the statements that later argued about the naming itself.

The scientific record is in `climate-science-evolution/02-problems/P08-observational-record.md` (§"The 1998–2012 slowdown (2009–2017)", status `RESOLVED` 2015–17) and is not re-argued here.

## Sides

`S-n`: statements from the sceptical side (and from outlets carrying them). `M-n`: statements from the mainstream side — scientists, assessments, agencies — including the mainstream's adoption of the word and its later self-criticism. The sides are about *who is speaking*, not about who is right; several M statements are mainstream scientists disagreeing with each other.

**M-9** — Lewandowsky, Oreskes, Risbey, Newell & Smithson (2015), "Seepage: Climate change denial and its effect on the scientific community," *Global Environmental Change* 33, 1–13, abstract. `[W]` from the UWA repository record.

**M-10** — Fyfe, Meehl, England et al. (2016), "Making sense of the early-2000s warming slowdown," *Nature Climate Change* 6, 224–228, opening. `[W]`

> It has been claimed that the early-2000s global warming slowdown or hiatus, characterized by a reduced rate of global surface warming, has been overstated, lacks sound scientific basis, or is unsupported by observations. The evidence presented here contradicts these claims.

**M-11** — Lewandowsky, Risbey & Oreskes (2016), "The 'pause' in global warming: Turning a routine fluctuation into a problem for science," *BAMS* 97(5), 723–733, abstract. `[W]` from the UWA repository record.

> There has been much recent published research about a putative 'pause' or 'hiatus' in global warming. We show that there are frequent fluctuations in the rate of warming around a longer-term warming trend, and that there is no evidence that identifies the recent period as unique or particularly unusual. In confirmation, we show that the notion of a pause in warming is considered to be misleading in a blind expert test. Nonetheless, the most recent fluctuation about the longer-term trend has been regarded by many as an explanatory challenge that climate science must resolve. This departs from long-standing practice, insofar as scientists have long recognized that the climate fluctuates, that linear increases in CO2 do not produce linear trends in global warming, and that 15-yr (or shorter) periods are not diagnostic of long-term trends. We suggest that the repetition of the 'warming has paused' message by contrarians was adopted by the scientific community in its problem-solving and answer-seeking role and has led to undue focus on, and mislabeling of, a recent fluctuation. We present an alternative framing that could have avoided inadvertently reinforcing a misleading claim.

**M-12** — Medhaug, Stolpe, Fischer & Knutti (2017), "Reconciling controversies about the 'global warming hiatus'," *Nature* 545, 41–47, abstract. `[W]`

> Between about 1998 and 2012, a time that coincided with political negotiations for preventing climate change, the surface of Earth seemed hardly to warm. This phenomenon, often termed the 'global warming hiatus', caused doubt in the public mind about how well anthropogenic climate change and natural variability are understood. Here we show that apparently contradictory conclusions stem from different definitions of 'hiatus' and from different datasets. A combination of changes in forcing, uptake of heat by the oceans, natural variability and incomplete observational coverage reconciles models and data. Combined with stronger recent warming trends in newer datasets, we are now more confident than ever that human influence is dominant in long-term warming.

**M-13** — NOAA Climate.gov, "Did global warming stop in 1998?", Rebecca Lindsey, 4 September 2018 (reviewed by Trenberth, Donner, Rood, Medhaug). `[W]`

In scientific journals and assessment reports, climate experts described the episode as a 'pause' or 'hiatus' in the previous decades' rapid warming: they knew it wouldn't last.
